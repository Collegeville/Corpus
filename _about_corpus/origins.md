For 28 years, Mike Heroux taught CSCI 373, the Senior Research Seminar capstone course at the
College of St. Benedict and St. John's University — an alternative to an Honors Thesis, built
around a single question: can you become the campus expert on a topic of your own choosing, and
build the habits to do it again on your own for the rest of your career?

Twenty-eight years of a course accumulates a lot. Assignments get rewritten as the field changes.
Slide decks get replaced. Techniques get refined, some get dropped, some get renamed. What
survives all of that turnover is a smaller, sturdier core: the actual techniques for doing
research, writing, and speaking well — the parts that were never really about any one semester's
students, or any one year's tools.

Corpus exists to pull that core out and give it a home that outlives the course itself. Mike is
retiring from teaching CS373; a successor instructor is taking over, and she needed something more
durable to build on than a personal website's file structure. Corpus is that something: an
archive, organized so the timeless material (Human Skills) is clearly separated from the material
that's genuinely tied to the current moment's tools (AI-Assisted Workflows and Agent Skills) — see
[Philosophy]({{ '/about-corpus/philosophy/' | relative_url }}) for why that split matters — with a visible path for
future instructors, students, and contributors to keep adding to it after this handoff.

The name fits a pattern already set by two related projects, Scribe (writing) and Orator
(speaking), both part of the Collegeville project's Latin naming convention, itself a nod to the
Benedictine setting the course was taught in for nearly three decades. *Corpus* — a body of work —
seemed like the right name for a site whose entire purpose is holding a body of work together
after the person who built it moves on to the next thing.
