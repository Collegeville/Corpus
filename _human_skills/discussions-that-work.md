## Definition

Most technical information exchange and decision-making happens in and through group
discussion — not through documents read in isolation. Good discussion skills, and an awareness
of group dynamics, directly shape how effective and how pleasant a work environment is. This
isn't a soft skill layered on top of the real work; for most people, it *is* most of the real
work.

## Learning Outcome

After using this technique, you should be able to run or participate in a technical discussion
that reaches a real decision, respects everyone's time, and leaves a record — rather than one
that circles without resolution or gets dominated by whoever talks the most.

## Core Structure

**Six ground rules for any discussion:**

1. **Come prepared.** Showing up unprepared wastes everyone else's time, not just your own.
2. **Show respect for others, always.** Every participant should be encouraged to speak, even
   if only to say "I have no opinion." Contention should be clarified thoroughly — it's often
   a misunderstanding, not a real disagreement. Critique targets ideas, never the person.
3. **Arrange participants so everyone can see everyone else.** Physical (or virtual) layout
   affects whether people actually engage.
4. **Thoroughly discuss important technical ideas.** Misunderstanding usually comes from an
   incomplete or inconsistent grasp of terms, not real disagreement. For genuinely important
   ideas, aim for the point where you can restate them in another participant's own words.
5. **Reach consensus when possible.** If the discussion is meant to produce a decision, work
   toward one everyone can live with.
6. **Record decisions.** Someone should write down what was actually decided — a good
   discussion that isn't recorded evaporates the moment the meeting ends.

**A supporting discipline for how you personally participate:**

- Think before you speak.
- Listen carefully to what others say.
- Don't interrupt.
- Build on what others have said when it's your turn.
- Only say what you actually believe.
- Don't stay silent — contribute.
- Don't dominate — once you've spoken, let at least two others speak before you speak again.
- Support a good idea even when it isn't yours.
- Keep searching for the best solution, even if it's different from what you thought going in.

## Worked Example

A team is deciding between two logging libraries. Applying the rules: each person comes having
already skimmed both libraries' docs (rule 1). Someone raises a concern about performance
overhead; before treating it as a disagreement, the group clarifies exactly what "overhead"
means to the person who raised it — and it turns out to be a misunderstanding about which mode
they'd actually run in (rule 4, resolving contention through clarification). Two people initially
favor different libraries, but one changes their position after hearing an argument they hadn't
considered — genuinely searching for the best answer rather than defending their initial pick.
The group reaches consensus on one library, and someone writes down the decision and the reason
for it before the meeting ends (rules 5 and 6) — so three weeks later, nobody has to re-litigate
why.

## Common Pitfalls

- Treating "I have no opinion" as not worth saying — silence is often mistaken for agreement.
- Debating a technical point for a while before realizing both sides actually meant different
  things by the same term.
- One or two people dominating, with quieter participants never actually weighing in.
- Reaching a decision in the room and never writing it down — three weeks later, no one
  remembers exactly what was agreed or why.
- Treating "supporting a good idea that isn't mine" as a loss, rather than as the actual goal of
  the discussion.

## Rubric / Checklist

- [ ] Everyone came prepared
- [ ] Every participant had a real opportunity to speak, including to say "no opinion"
- [ ] Apparent disagreements were checked for underlying misunderstanding before being treated
      as real disagreements
- [ ] Critique stayed focused on ideas, not people
- [ ] The discussion reached consensus, if a decision was the goal
- [ ] The decision (and its reasoning) was written down by someone in the room
