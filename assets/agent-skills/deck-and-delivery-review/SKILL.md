Review an uploaded slide deck against the combined guidance of two companion techniques on the
Corpus site: Slides That Work
(https://collegeville.github.io/Corpus/human-skills/slides-that-work/ — deck-building order and
slide content discipline) and Presentations That Work
(https://collegeville.github.io/Corpus/human-skills/presentations-that-work/ — story structure,
scope, and delivery readiness). This is a single-pass assessment, not an interview —
produce one complete report from the deck as given, rather than asking the author questions
first. If the author's message already includes context the deck can't show on its own (an
intended time limit, speaker notes, the venue), use it; otherwise note plainly where its absence
limits the assessment, rather than guessing.

## Step 1: Read the deck

Read every slide: title text, body content (bullets, prose, data, diagrams, quotes), and any
speaker notes present. Note the total slide count and, if a time limit was given, roughly what
pace that implies.

## Step 2: Identify the apparent structure

Before scoring anything, work out what the deck is actually doing:
- Is there an explicit closing "key points" or summary slide? What does it claim as the
  takeaways?
- Is there an opening outline/agenda slide, and does it preview the same points the closing
  slide lands on? (A mismatch here is itself worth flagging — it suggests the intro wasn't
  written last, against the guidance.)
- Which slides are "middle" content, and which of the identified takeaways does each one
  actually support?

This structural read is what the rest of the report is scored against — don't re-derive it
per checklist item.

## Step 3: Score against the combined checklist

Organize the report into these five themes (combining both source pages' checklists rather than
listing them as two separate raw lists):

**Takeaways & structure**
- 3–5 key takeaways are identifiable, including one that synthesizes the rest
- Opening and closing points match (evidence the intro was built to fit the talk, not guessed
  at first)
- The talk has a real beginning, middle, and end — a story, not a flat list

**Slide content discipline**
- Slides carry data, diagrams, and quotes — not a transcript of what would be spoken
- Slide titles state the point being made, not just a topic label
- A big-picture diagram or mental model is present somewhere, if the material supports one

**Scope & focus**
- Every middle slide connects to a specific takeaway; anything that doesn't, flag it by slide
  number
- Scope looks realistic for the apparent time available — advertising the underlying work
  rather than trying to replace it

**Title & framing**
- Title is compelling and specific, not generic
- For longer decks, some mechanism exists to remind the audience where they are in the story
  (a revisited outline slide, section dividers, etc.)

**Delivery readiness — flag as self-assess, don't guess**
Several checklist items genuinely can't be verified from a static deck: practiced timing, a
backup plan for technical failure, anticipated audience questions, and dress code. List these
explicitly as items the deck alone can't confirm, rather than scoring them or silently omitting
them.

For each scored item: mark it **Pass**, **Concern**, or **Can't assess from deck**, with one to
two sentences of evidence tied to a specific slide number or title — not a generic restatement
of the guideline.

## Step 4: Close with priorities

End the report with:
1. The top 3 highest-impact fixes, ranked, in plain language — not just a repeat of every
   "Concern" flagged above.
2. The explicit self-assess list from Step 3, framed as "verify these yourself before
   presenting," so the author knows the report is deliberately incomplete on those points
   rather than having missed them.
